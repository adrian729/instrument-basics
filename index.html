Every fingering for the flute

Every note of the usual range, from the lowest up: the standard fingering first, then the alternatives, each with what it's for. Choose the instrument with the switches above the chart.

  • The foot joint gives the lowest notes: the right pinky's rollers for low C♯ and C. Most flutes have this C foot; a B foot adds a key for low B, and often the gizmo key, which helps top C.
  • It sounds as written. The concert flute is in C: the note on the page is the note you hear.
  • The right pinky stays down on the E♭ key for almost every note. The diagrams show the few where it's lifted or moved, as for low D, middle D and the foot joint's notes.

Reading the diagrams

  • The flute seen from above as you play it: the mouthpiece on the left, the left hand nearer it, the right hand further out.
  • Filled is pressed, outlined is up. The numbers are your fingers: 1 the index, 2 the middle, 3 the ring finger.
  • Above the body, the left pinky's G♯. Below it, the keys on your side of the instrument: the thumb's two (use B; a few fingerings ask for the B♭ lever), the right hand's side lever and trill keys, and the right pinky's E♭ with the foot joint's rollers for low C♯ and C.
  • The foot joint's B key and gizmo are a B foot's: on a C foot, use the fingerings without them.
  • The dashed D and D♯ are the trill keys' touches, between your right-hand fingers, pressed with whichever finger is free. The side B♭ lever, by right 1, is pressed with the side of that finger. Only a few notes use these.
  • Under each diagram the same fingering in words. Open-hole and closed-hole flutes are fingered alike.

Most alternatives are for tuning, or for soft and loud playing, or to make a note speak, or for a fast slur. Try them with a tuner, and keep what works on yours.

The fingerings are from The Woodwind Fingering Guide, their comments adapted: its flute pages (first octave, second, third, fourth) and piccolo pages (first octave, second, third).
