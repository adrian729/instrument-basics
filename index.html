Every fingering for the flute and the piccolo

Every note from the bottom to the top, the standard fingering first and the alternatives under it, for tuning, soft or loud playing, and response. For the Boehm flute and piccolo, fingered alike for two octaves: choose yours with the switch above the chart.

  • The foot joint gives the lowest notes: the right pinky's rollers for low C♯ and C. Most flutes have this C foot; a B foot adds a key for low B, and often the gizmo key, which helps top C.
  • It sounds as written. The concert flute is in C: the note on the page is the note you hear.
  • The right pinky stays down on the E♭ key for almost every note. The diagrams show the few where it's lifted or moved, as for low D, middle D and the foot joint's notes.
  • There's no foot joint. No piccolo has one: its lowest note is D, all six fingers down, and the flute's low C♯, C and B keys aren't needed for anything.
  • It sounds an octave higher. Piccolo music is written an octave below what's heard. The chart names written notes, and each card says what you'll hear.
  • The right pinky stays down on the E♭ key for almost every note. The diagrams show the few where it's lifted, as for low D and middle D.

Reading the diagrams

  • The flutepiccolo seen from above as you play it: the mouthpiece on the left, the left hand nearer it, the right hand further out.
  • Dark is pressed, white is up. The numbers are your fingers: 1 the index, 2 the middle, 3 the ring finger.
  • Above the body, the left pinky's G♯. Below it, the keys on your side of the instrument: the thumb's two (use B; a few fingerings ask for the B♭ lever), the right hand's side lever and trill keys, and the right pinky's E♭ with the foot joint's rollers for low C♯ and C.
  • The foot joint's B key and gizmo are a B foot's: on a C foot, use the fingerings without them.
  • The dashed D and D♯ are the trill keys' touches, between your right-hand fingers, pressed with whichever finger is free. The side B♭ lever, by right 1, is pressed with the side of that finger. Only a few notes use these.
  • Under each diagram the same fingering in words. Open-hole and closed-hole flutespiccolos are fingered alike.

Most alternatives are for tuning, or for soft and loud playing, or to make a note speak, or for a fast slur. Try them with a tuner, and keep what works on yours.

The piccolo tends to be sharp low down and flat at the very top: most alternatives are for that, or for soft and loud playing, or to make a note speak. Try them with a tuner, and keep what works on yours.
